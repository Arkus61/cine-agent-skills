# Screenplay Craft

Use this reference to turn approved outline events into performable, visual scenes. It is an original synthesis of general craft principles, not a substitute for project-specific authorship.

## Scene engine

Define four things before writing dialogue:

1. **Objective:** what one participant must make happen now, expressed as an achievable verb.
2. **Conflict:** the person, condition, evidence gap, or incompatible objective that resists progress.
3. **Tactics:** the observable methods used when the first approach fails.
4. **Turn:** the decision, discovery, cost, or changed relationship that makes the exit condition different from the entrance.

A scene need not end in victory. It must alter knowledge, leverage, commitment, risk, or available action. Preserve the outline's event function; do not add spectacle merely to simulate a turn.

## Visual action and playable behavior

Write what can be photographed or heard in the present tense. Replace labels with evidence:

- not "she is anxious," but a repeated attempt to align a key with the wrong lock;
- not "he wants control," but his moving the only chair before the guest enters;
- not "they distrust each other," but each refusing to release the same document.

Use playable verbs that give a performer a tactic: test, charm, stall, corner, bargain, shame, recruit, conceal, expose, soothe, provoke, refuse. Avoid directing an unobservable inner state. Objects, distance, entrances, exits, and withheld physical actions can carry story without dialogue.

Action paragraphs create reading and screen rhythm. Keep one meaningful action progression per paragraph. Break when focus, tactic, or consequence changes. Dense description should earn its duration through necessary staging or evidence.

## Objective, conflict, and turn on the page

Enter after the routine has become pressure. Establish location and present action, then let a participant pursue something. Escalate by narrowing options, increasing cost, or revealing incompatible aims—not by repeating the same refusal louder.

At each exchange ask:

- What is the speaker trying to make the other person do?
- What new information or behavior changes the next tactic?
- What can the audience infer before it is stated?
- Which beat would disappear without changing the outcome? Cut or combine it.

Leave once the changed condition is legible. A final image, withheld response, or completed action can carry the turn more economically than a summary line.

## Subtext and dialogue economy

Dialogue is action. A line may probe, distract, demand, bargain, defend, reframe, or conceal. Let the literal topic and the immediate aim differ when the situation supports subtext. Do not make opacity a default: the audience still needs enough context to follow the choice.

Economy means removing duplication, not making every line short. Cut:

- greetings and logistics that do not create pressure;
- information both speakers already know solely for the audience;
- a line that explains the action immediately before or after it;
- repeated names, emotional labels, and conclusions the image supplies;
- parentheticals that prescribe a result instead of clarifying necessary action.

Read exchanges aloud. Distinguish voices through priorities, syntax, avoidance, precision, rhythm, and what each person notices—not phonetic caricature or decorative catchphrases.

## Exposition through pressure

Reveal background when it changes the present negotiation. Make information:

- a price for cooperation;
- a fact one participant tests or disputes;
- an instruction whose omission has consequences;
- evidence that changes responsibility;
- a secret whose partial disclosure shifts leverage.

Distribute information across action and reaction. Do not withhold a fact every participant would naturally use merely to create a later reveal. For nonfiction, distinguish verified fact, participant assertion, question, interpretation, and unknown outcome.

## Rhythm

Build contrast across and within scenes:

- long action followed by a precise line;
- interruption followed by a held silence;
- rapid tactics followed by a physical consequence;
- public language followed by private behavior;
- expected payoff followed by a cost that redirects the next scene.

Pacing is the rate of meaningful change, not the number of cuts or words. A quiet scene can move quickly when leverage changes; an action scene can stall when events repeat without consequence.

## Format routes

### Feature, short, and series fiction

Treat the unit outline as the coverage contract. Features and shorts require whole-script closure appropriate to their supplied form. A series episode resolves its local objective while preserving only approved continuing dependencies. Use cold opens, act-outs, or tags only when the supplied delivery format requires real presentation boundaries.

### Documentary and interview-led work

Do not write imagined interview answers as dialogue. Script confirmed narration only from supplied, attributable facts. Represent planned access, questions, observations, archive, and verification steps as conditional where outcomes are unknown.

An interview sequence can still have objective, conflict, and turn:

- objective: test a documented claim or understand a participant's account;
- conflict: incomplete records, disagreement, access limits, memory, or a credible alternative explanation;
- tactics: open question, specific follow-up, document comparison, pause, clarification;
- turn: a verified contradiction, a bounded new lead, an acknowledged uncertainty, or a changed next reporting step.

Preserve the context of answers and the relationship between a question and quotation. Never stage an event as actuality or predetermine a participant into a villain, hero, confession, or revelation.

### Commercial

Make the audience tension, claim, proof, and action legible inside runtime. Do not invent product performance, testimonials, comparative claims, legal clearance, or research. A visible demonstration must show only what supplied evidence supports.

### Music video

Choose performance, narrative, conceptual, or hybrid logic from the approved outline. Build turns from performance state, visual rule, spatial change, motif, or narrative action. Do not force explanatory dialogue or a feature-style arc.

### Short-form

Orient quickly, create a specific reason to continue, and pay off within the declared duration. Retention comes from visible progress, changing pressure, comparison, or a fair information gap—not confusion, false claims, or withheld basic context.

## Revision and metadata discipline

Keep the Fountain page clean for readers and performers. Put exact project, unit, scene, event, character, location, objective, conflict, turn, and setup/payoff mappings in the sibling JSON. If a heading changes, update its metadata text exactly. If an approved event changes, revise upstream rather than hiding the change in screenplay prose.

Audit each pass in this order:

1. event and evidence fidelity;
2. objective, tactics, conflict, and turn;
3. visual action and staging clarity;
4. subtext, exposition, and dialogue economy;
5. rhythm and format-specific completion;
6. IDs, headings, cues, and setup/payoff mappings.
