# Animation performance

Build performance from observable changes in attention, objective, obstacle, tactic, contact, and recovery. Preserve supplied directing intent before proposing detail.

## Source intent before invention

Start by copying the supplied character, scene, shot, camera, duration, timebase, and performance records into `source_context`. A performance plan interprets those records; it does not replace them. Bind each shot-character plan to one supplied camera-intent ID and the performance-intent ID for that character in that shot.

Build an explicit `supplied_facts` ledger inside each source shot before drafting downstream plans. Each record contains one exact value and exact source reference plus an applicability scope. Use `shot` with a null character for the downstream camera relationship. Use `shot-character` with the applicable character ID for repeated action, performance, pose, readability, expression, dialogue or no-dialogue, and continuity claims. Two characters in the same shot need separate records even when their wording happens to match, and a record from one shot never authorizes a claim in another.

Every downstream field marked `supplied` must repeat both parts of an applicable ledger record exactly. A matching value with a different reference is not bound; neither is a matching reference with altered wording. Do not add provenance to the ledger or use downstream claims to justify ledger entries: the ledger is copied from source material and is deliberately outside downstream provenance traversal. Reject duplicate records rather than treating repetition as additional evidence. Inferred and proposed decisions cite their basis but do not need an exact ledger match.

Use provenance consistently:

- **supplied** repeats an established source fact and cites its exact source location;
- **inferred** states a necessary consequence of supplied facts while naming the reasoning;
- **proposed** introduces a bounded animation choice for review;
- **approved** is allowed only when approval is actually supplied.

Put missing dialogue, anatomy, articulation, state, or approval in assumptions or uncertainties. Do not turn absence into a creative fact.

## Acting beats and poses

Give each acting beat one playable change and a numeric interval. Use key poses to state the clearest body relationship at entries, decisions, contacts, reversals, and exits. A pose is readable when silhouette, line of action, gaze or orientation, balance, and important contact remain legible in the supplied frame.

Name the action and its performance function separately. “Raises a hand” describes action; “tests whether the other character will flinch” gives it playable purpose. Favor the minimum number of beats that makes each change of thought, tactic, contact, or state reviewable.

For each key pose, check:

- the pose belongs to one acting beat and occurs inside that beat;
- the support, balance, or mechanical constraint is understandable;
- the important face, appendage, prop, or contact is visible at the supplied shot size;
- the pose differs meaningfully from adjacent keys;
- entry and exit keys preserve supplied continuity.

A key pose is not a requirement to make every channel extreme. Restrained work may read through an eye-line change, a pressure shift at contact, or a small reorganization of balance.

## Staging and camera

Stage for the supplied composition rather than an imaginary neutral view. Protect the information the camera must see, including eye direction, hands, feet, props, contact points, and negative space. A locked camera can make small changes powerful; a moving camera may require simpler pose changes and protected framing. Animation direction does not silently revise the camera plan.

Treat screen size as an information limit. A detail that cannot be seen in the supplied framing cannot carry the only evidence of a beat. When the camera moves, coordinate the timing of pose changes with when the relevant relationship becomes visible; do not compensate by inventing a camera move.

## Multiple characters

Give every applicable character its own shot-character plan, IDs, performance binding, poses, and continuity state even when several characters share a shot. Coordinate shared contact and eyelines through matching timing and acceptance criteria, but do not merge ownership. A beat or pose from one character must never be referenced as if it belonged to another.

## Restraint

Small performance is not inactivity. Shift attention before posture, let breath or contact pressure carry internal change, and use purposeful holds. Avoid adding face, hand, or body noise merely to keep the character moving.

An active hold sustains an intention. It may contain breathing, balance correction, changing contact pressure, material response, or another subject-appropriate residual trace. The trace should support the beat, not compete with it. Preserve stillness when it focuses the audience on supplied dialogue, subtext, or a reaction.

## Subject-appropriate expression

For a human or human-faced subject, coordinate eyes, lids, brows, cheeks, jaw, mouth, breath, and head without making every channel peak together. For a nonhuman subject, use only its supplied expressive structure: orientation, appendage configuration, volume, emission, material flex, or locomotion. Mark inapplicable human anatomy explicitly.

Separate attention, thought, effort, and speech. Eyes or orientation can establish attention before the head or body follows. Effort may affect breath, jaw, contact, or whole-body organization. Do not assume that a human face is the default carrier of emotion, and do not describe absent anatomy as though it were a rig limitation.

## Lip-sync intent

Record whether dialogue synchronization is specified, absent, or anatomically not applicable. When specified, preserve supplied words, intention, phrasing, breath, and shot readability; avoid treating mouth shapes as a substitute for acting. Do not invent dialogue or recording timing.

For dialogue, mark thought changes, pauses, stress, interruption, and the final intended state before detailing mouth behavior. Coordinate eyes, face, head, posture, and hands around the line rather than moving them continuously. Selective holds and clear changes often read better than equal motion on every sound. Any phonetic or mouth-shape detail must remain subordinate to supplied performance and subject anatomy.

## Continuity and transitions

Record a numbered continuity state for each shot-character plan. Its entry and exit states should describe observable pose, orientation, contact, expression, prop, and motion conditions that matter to adjacent coverage. Link each later state to the immediately previous state for that character. Continuity order follows the supplied shot sequence; it is not permission to revise editorial order.

## Review

Acceptance criteria should be visible in playback: the intended turn reads, poses remain clear at shot size, contacts feel intentional, face or orientation supports the beat, camera framing is preserved, and entry/exit states match continuity.

Write criteria as evidence, not taste. Useful criteria identify what a reviewer can see and which supplied intent it protects. Examples include: the attention change precedes the hand action; the planted foot does not drift before lift-off; the final word lands during the held eye line; the nonhuman subject communicates alarm through supplied appendages rather than invented brows; and the exit contact matches the next shot.

Reject a plan when it broadens a restrained performance, changes supplied dialogue or action, hides a key beat outside the frame, gives a character another character's graph records, or treats a proposal as approval.
