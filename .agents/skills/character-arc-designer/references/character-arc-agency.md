# Character Arc and Agency

Use this reference to turn story events into playable evidence of character movement. It provides analytical choices, not a compulsory transformation formula.

## Want and need

An external objective, or want, is the result a character knowingly pursues in the story world: escape, solve the case, protect a person, win access, conceal evidence, or preserve an institution. It should create selectable actions and let the structure answer whether the pursuit advances, changes, or fails.

An internal need is the capacity, value, truth, relationship, or mode of action under deeper test. It is not automatically virtue, therapy, self-knowledge, or a lesson delivered by the writer. A character may:

- discover that the objective and need conflict;
- use the objective to avoid the need;
- pursue both in a new way;
- preserve a sound value while changing others;
- knowingly refuse the need;
- remain genuinely unresolved at the supplied endpoint.

Test the distinction with behavior. If two fields would produce the same staged action in every event, rewrite one. "Wants safety" and "needs security" are synonyms; "wants to close the case" and "needs to trust evidence that weakens their authority" create different choices.

## Agency and choice under pressure

Agency is the character's capacity to affect outcomes through selection among meaningful options. Power is not required. A prisoner can bargain, conceal, cooperate, signal, refuse, reinterpret, sacrifice, or redirect another person's choice. Conversely, a powerful character has weak dramatic agency if every outcome arrives by accident or instruction.

Build agency with three linked elements:

1. **Capacity:** Name what the character can perceive, decide, risk, withhold, grant, build, expose, or change.
2. **Constraint:** Name what genuinely limits the options: incomplete knowledge, social duty, physical barriers, time, unequal power, prior promises, or conflicting values.
3. **Choice under pressure:** Present at least two credible costs, let the character choose, and trace the consequence into a later event or relationship.

Do not mistake reaction for choice. Being attacked supplies pressure; staying to protect evidence, fleeing with an ally, or using the attack to conceal a betrayal supplies agency. Do not erase constraints to make a character appear "strong." Pressure is useful when it makes values legible.

## Arc shapes

Assign a shape from event evidence, not prestige or protagonist status.

### Positive

The character increasingly enacts the internal need through costly choices. Progress need not be smooth: relapse, tactical failure, and delayed recognition can make the endpoint more credible. The endpoint must demonstrate a changed choice pattern, not merely announce insight.

### Flat

The character's governing value or tested truth remains substantially stable. Pressure clarifies its cost and limits while the character changes another person, system, inquiry, or field of action. A flat arc is not a static character: tactics, knowledge, relationships, and consequences still move.

### Negative

The character increasingly doubles down on a damaging strategy, misuses a real strength, or rejects the need. Do not equate suffering, defeat, death, disability, marginalization, or unconventional values with a negative arc. The evidence is a worsening choice pattern and its consequences.

### Open

The supplied events create meaningful tests but do not settle direction, or the work intentionally preserves an unresolved endpoint. Open does not mean "unfinished notes." State what has changed, what remains contested, and what evidence would distinguish later directions.

## Behavioral evidence and contradiction

Screen character is inferred from what can be seen and heard: action, speech, silence, timing, use of objects, spatial choices, exchanges, refusals, and consequences. The Living Handbook of Narratology distinguishes direct trait assignment from indirect characterization inferred through narrative evidence. An open film-studies text likewise emphasizes that audiovisual character development must be expressed through physical behavior and dialogue. Apply both principles by replacing labels with evidence.

Weak: "She is loyal but conflicted."

Playable: "She reports her brother's theft, then deletes the camera angle that would identify him."

A contradiction is not random inconsistency. Construct it from at least three parts:

- a declared value, self-description, or habitual strategy;
- conduct that conflicts with it under a specific pressure;
- a dramatic consequence that changes knowledge, trust, power, risk, or future choice.

Contradictions can expose self-deception, competing loyalties, limited knowledge, tactical compromise, or genuine change. Do not use diagnostic shorthand to explain them. Describe the conduct and context; leave interpretation proportionate to the evidence.

## Relationship arcs

A relationship is a changing system between characters, not a note attached only to the protagonist. Track it directionally because each side may understand and value it differently.

Use five variables when useful:

| Variable | Observable movement |
|---|---|
| Trust | Evidence is shared, tested, withheld, or believed |
| Power | Permission, leverage, dependence, or decision rights change |
| Knowledge | One side learns, conceals, misreads, or verifies something about the other |
| Intimacy | Vulnerability, access, touch, language, or distance changes |
| Obligation | A promise, debt, duty, rescue, betrayal, or boundary changes future choices |

Give each side an objective independent of servicing the protagonist's growth. A mentor, rival, partner, child, victim, or ally should be able to change an option or outcome. Relationship movement becomes convincing when mutual choices alter what each can ask, risk, or refuse.

## Ensemble function

An ensemble earns its size when characters perform distinct dramatic work. Useful functions include:

- pursuing a competing route to the same outcome;
- carrying a separate plotline or source of knowledge;
- embodying a viable countervalue without becoming a straw figure;
- forcing a choice the protagonist could otherwise avoid;
- bearing a consequence that changes group strategy;
- preserving institutional, communal, or historical stakes beyond one lead.

Do not duplicate functions under different names. Compare objectives, access to information, pressure choices, and relationship leverage. Merge characters whose removal changes no event or decision, unless their recurrence has a deliberate world or production function.

Serial ensembles can occupy different tiers while remaining legible. Jason Mittell's open-access *Complex TV* describes serial characters as part of an ongoing, dynamically recognized ensemble whose roles can remain meaningful across episodes. Operationally, give recurring characters stable identifying functions but allow alliances, focus, and relative leverage to move.

## Serial pacing

Do not spend the entire internal arc at the pilot or reset it after every episode. Pace serial movement at three scales:

1. **Event turn:** A choice changes immediate tactics, knowledge, or relationship conditions.
2. **Unit turn:** Accumulated event consequences create a provisional new stance, obligation, or strategy.
3. **Season or long-form turn:** Repeated choices establish, overturn, or leave open the governing pattern.

Vary the kind of movement. One unit may test competence, another trust, another public cost. Close local questions while retaining a larger pressure. A relapse must arise from new pressure, consequence, or competing value; otherwise it reads as a convenience reset. A late transformation should have earlier behavioral precursors. A flat arc still accumulates cost and effect. A negative arc still requires credible alternatives the character could have chosen.

## Non-diagnostic boundary

Do not infer or assign a clinical condition from behavior, contradiction, trauma, eccentricity, conflict, or harmful choices. Do not treat diagnostic vocabulary as a synonym for dangerous, irrational, villainous, unreliable, or unusual. If a qualified, supplied story source establishes a medical fact, preserve its provenance in the appropriate canon or source material; this artifact still describes only story function, objective, need, agency, observed conduct, relationship effects, and consequences.

This boundary applies to real people and fictional characters. It protects both accuracy and dramatic usefulness: a concrete choice under concrete pressure is more playable than an unsupported label.
