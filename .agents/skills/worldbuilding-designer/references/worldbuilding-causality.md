# Worldbuilding causality

Use this reference to turn story-facing facts into a coherent world without mistaking volume for depth. A useful world bible predicts pressure: when a condition changes, it clarifies who can act, what they risk, and what follows.

## 1. Begin with the story boundary

Worldbuilding scale is a production and narrative decision. Map only the scales that can affect the supplied story:

| Scale | Useful questions |
|---|---|
| Personal | What can a person access, carry, know, owe, or survive? |
| Household or group | What is shared, rationed, inherited, hidden, or enforced? |
| Location | What material conditions shape movement, labor, shelter, and risk? |
| Institution | Who authorizes, records, excludes, repairs, punishes, or distributes? |
| Region | What travels between locations: people, goods, energy, disease, belief, or information? |
| Ecology | What energy and matter enter, circulate, accumulate, or leave? |
| History | Which past event still changes present claims, infrastructure, memory, or behavior? |

Do not fill an atlas because a location exists. State unknown geography and unreachable scales as excluded or uncertain. A bounded world can feel larger when characters encounter incomplete maps, indirect effects, incompatible accounts, and costly travel; those signals are more useful than uncaused names.

## 2. Build the causal spine

Use the sequence below for each important world element:

1. **Condition:** Start from supplied evidence or mark a new condition proposed.
2. **Constraint:** Identify scarcity, delay, danger, uncertainty, access, maintenance, or dependence.
3. **Adaptation:** Describe an observable practice, tool, norm, route, institution, or strategy.
4. **Distribution:** Identify who controls the adaptation, who benefits, and who pays.
5. **Second-order effect:** Ask what the adaptation changes elsewhere.
6. **Story consequence:** Connect the chain to a choice, conflict, clue, reversal, or outcome.

Example pattern: seasonal flooding is the condition; safe crossings are scarce; residents maintain raised paths; a gatekeeping office controls repair access; peripheral routes decay first; a character must choose between official delay and an unsafe crossing. The raised path's color or ornament can follow later.

## 3. Put constraints before decoration

A constraint is not automatically misery. It creates a design surface.

- Scarcity can produce rationing, substitution, reuse, smuggling, prestige, theft, or collective care.
- Distance can produce relay systems, local autonomy, delayed knowledge, seasonal migration, or uneven law.
- Maintenance can produce skilled roles, inspection rituals, spare-part markets, and vulnerability to neglected infrastructure.
- Uncertainty can produce experiments, taboos, insurance, rumor, archives, or rival expertise.
- Dangerous abundance can produce containment, licensing, protective customs, waste, or exploitation.

Choose adaptations that characters can encounter. Avoid adding five institutions where one repeated access decision explains the pressure.

## 4. Trace material and ecological logic

Track energy and matter separately. Energy enters, changes form, becomes unavailable for some uses, and leaves; matter moves, accumulates, is transformed, or is recycled. For a story world, ask:

- What provides food, water, heat, light, power, shelter, and repair materials?
- What labor, land, time, or infrastructure keeps those flows available?
- What waste, heat, residue, extraction damage, or displaced risk follows?
- Which organisms, landscapes, or communities depend on the same flows?
- What happens when a flow is interrupted, intensified, redirected, or monopolized?

Do not borrow a scientific term to decorate an impossible mechanism. A speculative exception may break real-world expectations, but its new boundary must be explicit and consistently consequential.

Ecological scale matters. A local organism does not establish a planetary biome. A single observed response does not establish a species-wide behavior. Distinguish observed range, proposed range, and unknown range.

## 5. Design social consequence

Every durable resource flow creates access questions. For a faction or institution, specify:

- the result it seeks;
- the resource, knowledge, legitimacy, or force it can use;
- the dependencies that can interrupt its authority;
- the formal and informal rules it enforces;
- the groups that benefit;
- the groups that carry cost, exclusion, exposure, or delay;
- the choice or conflict this distribution creates in the story.

Power is relational. A council with a title but no means of enforcement, recordkeeping, material dependency, or public compliance has no demonstrated authority. Conversely, a maintenance crew may hold practical power even without formal rank because others depend on its knowledge.

## 6. Treat cultures as varied practices

Culture is not an aesthetic package or a list of traits. Connect values and norms to repeated action, language, artifacts, memory, and material conditions. Then include internal variation:

- Who follows the norm, and in which situations?
- Who interprets it differently?
- What competing value makes compliance difficult?
- Does status change who may violate or redefine it?
- How do newcomers, border communities, generations, professions, or factions differ?

Avoid treating a named group as unanimous, ancient, exotic, or static. One ceremonial object does not prove a universal belief. One character's statement may be a belief, strategy, error, or lie rather than world fact.

## 7. Give history a present legacy

Include a historical entry only when its legacy is active. Useful legacies include:

- a law, debt, border, inheritance, stigma, alliance, or unresolved claim;
- infrastructure built for a former purpose;
- knowledge preserved, distorted, restricted, or lost;
- a recurring material hazard or ecological change;
- conflicting public and private memories.

Record the evidence basis. A legend can be canon as a legend without its content becoming canon history. A contested record can remain contested.

## 8. Use terminology as a control surface

A term should make distinctions stable across writers and departments. Define:

- what it names;
- what observable evidence justifies its use;
- what it does not imply;
- whether it is an in-world term, production term, or both.

For example, a working term for the observed edge of damage must not imply a mapped perimeter, single cause, or central intelligence unless evidence supports those claims.

## 9. Run the scale and consequence audit

Before handoff, ask:

1. Does each location have material conditions and access constraints?
2. Does each institution depend on something characters can affect or observe?
3. Does each culture include internal variation and material practice?
4. Does each historical event change the present?
5. Does each ecological or material claim state its known scale?
6. Can every major element be traced to a supplied fact or labeled proposal?
7. Does every retained detail change a story choice, risk, clue, relationship, or outcome?

Remove or defer details that fail the audit. Uncertainty is more reliable than ornamental certainty.
